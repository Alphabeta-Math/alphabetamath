## Statement

For a positive integer $n$, consider the ordered pairs $(p,q)$ of primes for which there exist integers $x,y$ with $n=p^2+q^2+x^2+y^2$ and at least one of $p=2$, $q=2$, $p\mid x$, $q\mid y$. There are at most $(12C+2)n^{13/16}$ such pairs, where $C=4\cdot16^{65536}$ and $n^{13/16}$ is the thirteenth power of the positive sixteenth root of $n$. Primes have only $1$ and themselves as positive divisors and are greater than $1$. The notation $d\mid t$ means $t=dk$ for an integer $k$.

## Facts & Assumptions

[F1] For each positive integer $m$, $r_2(m)$ counts the ordered integer pairs $(u,v)$ with $u^2+v^2=m$, and $r_2(m)\le C m^{1/16}$. [[math:def-two-square-representation-function]] [[lem-author-f93d28423212-two-square-multiplicity]]

[F2] For $n\ge1$ the number of integer pairs $(d,t)$ with $d>0$, $d\mid t$ and $d^2+t^2\le n$ is at most $6n^{3/4}$. [[lem-author-f93d28423212-divisible-coordinate-count]]

[F3] A Cartesian product consists of the ordered pairs from its two factors and is finite when both factors are finite. Every subset of a finite set is finite and has at most its size. A finite family of pairwise disjoint finite sets has finite union, with size equal to the sum of their sizes. A bijection is an injective and surjective map, and it transports finiteness and cardinality between the two sets. [[math:def-cartesian-product]] [[math:thm-product-rule]] [[math:thm-subset-of-a-finite-set]] [[math:thm-sum-rule]] [[math:def-injection-surjection-bijection]] [[math:def-finite-cardinality]]

[F4] Use the canonical identifications $\mathbb N\subseteq\mathbb Z\subseteq\mathbb Q\subseteq\mathbb R$, which preserve arithmetic and order. The naturals correspond to the nonnegative integers, and finite cardinalities in real comparisons are read through these identifications. For real $T$ the floor is the unique integer $h$ with $h\le T<h+1$, and no integer lies strictly between an integer and its successor. For each natural $h$, the initial segment $\{t\in\mathbb N:t<h\}$ is finite with cardinality $h$. [[math:def-natural-numbers]] [[math:def-nat-order]] [[math:def-rationals]] [[math:def-real-numbers]] [[math:lem-nat-embeds-int]] [[math:lem-int-embeds-rat]] [[math:lem-rat-embeds-dense]] [[math:lem-integer-part]] [[math:lem-cardinality-of-finite-natural-initial-segment]]

[F5] The integers are a totally ordered commutative ring under integer addition, multiplication and negation. Here an integer square $a^2$ is the product $a\cdot a$. [[math:def-int-operations]] [[math:thm-int-ordered-ring]]

[F6] The reals form an ordered field with the usual real order. For positive real bases use the rational power laws and monotonicity in the base for positive exponents and in the exponent for bases at least $1$. A rational $a/b$ is the class of $(a,b)$. Rational addition uses $(ad+cb,bd)$ for $a/b+c/d$, and for positive denominators the comparison $a/b<c/d$ is equivalent to $ad<cb$. Natural powers of real bases are repeated products, starting at $1$. [[math:thm-reals-ordered-field]] [[math:def-real-order]] [[math:def-rational-power]] [[math:lem-rational-power-laws]] [[math:lem-rational-power-monotone]] [[math:def-rationals]] [[math:def-rat-operations]] [[math:def-rat-order]] [[math:def-integer-power]]

[F7] Ordered pairs agree exactly when their respective coordinates agree. Write $(p,q,x,y)$ for the nested ordered pair $((p,x),(q,y))$, so equality of these quadruples is coordinatewise. Subset conditions use separation. A graph with a unique output for each input defines a function, and a family indexed by a set is a function assigning one member to each index. [[math:def-ordered-pair]] [[math:thm-the-characterising-property-of-ordered-pairs]] [[math:def-subset-and-proper-subset]] [[math:def-function]] [[math:def-indexed-family]]

[F8] A sum over a finite set of size $d$ is computed by enumerating that set via a bijection from the natural initial segment of length $d$. The natural and real prefix sums start at zero and add one term at a time. The natural-to-real comparison uses the canonical map $\iota$ given by $\iota(0)=0$ and $\iota(k+1)=\iota(k)+1$. This agrees with the composite in [F4] by induction, since the composite satisfies the same two equations. The image of a natural prefix sum is the real prefix sum of its images. For real sequences on $\mathbb N$, pointwise inequalities for $k<d$ imply the same inequality for their prefix sums, and the prefix sum of a constant $b$ is $\iota(d)b$. [[math:def-sum-over-a-finite-index-set]] [[math:def-nat-finite-sum-and-product]] [[math:def-finite-sum]] [[math:def-canonical-natural]] [[math:thm-induction-principle]] [[math:lem-nat-finite-sum-laws-and-the-canonical-embedding]] [[math:lem-finite-sum-laws]]

[F9] For any set $R$, its power set $\mathcal P(R)$ contains exactly the subsets of $R$. Sets with the same elements are equal. [[math:lem-the-power-set-of-a-set-is-a-set]] [[math:def-axiom-of-extensionality]]

[F10] A point belongs to the indexed union of a family exactly when it belongs to some member of that family. A point belongs to $A\cup B$ exactly when it belongs to at least one of $A,B$, and to $A\cap B$ exactly when it belongs to both. The sets $A$ and $B$ are disjoint when $A\cap B=\varnothing$, equivalently when they have no element in common because $\varnothing$ is the unique set with no elements. ## Proof

**Given:** A positive integer $n$.

**Goal:** Bound the stated number of prime pairs.

1.1 By integer discreteness $n\ge1$, so $U=n^{1/2}\ge1$. Set $h=\lfloor U\rfloor$ and let $P$ be the subset of $\mathbb Z$ of positive integers at most $U$. Since $h\le U<h+1$ and there is no integer between $h$ and $h+1$, the condition $a\le U$ for an integer $a$ is equivalent to $a\le h$. In particular $h\ge1$ since $1\le U$. The translation $a\mapsto a-1$ maps $P$ bijectively onto $\{t\in\mathbb N:t<h\}$ with inverse $t\mapsto t+1$, using $t<h$ if and only if $t+1\le h$ for integer $t$. Thus $P$ is finite, with $|P|=h\le U$. [given, F3, F4, F5, F6, F7]

1.2 Among representations with $p\mid x$, the realized pairs $(p,x)$ lie in the finite set from [F2] because $p>0$ and $p^2+x^2\le n$. For each such fixed pair, the residual $m=n-p^2-x^2$ is an integer by closure under the integer operations. A completion has $m=q^2+y^2>0$ because $q$ is prime, and $m\le n$ because $p^2+x^2\ge0$. Thus the completion pairs $(q,y)$ form a subset of the finite set counted by $r_2(m)$ for this positive integer $m$. Their number is at most $C m^{1/16}\le C n^{1/16}$. [given, F1, F2, F3, F4, F5, F6, F7]

2.1 In any representation from the Statement, every square is nonnegative in the integer order, so $p^2,q^2\le n$. Both primes are positive, so comparison of positive square roots in the canonical real embedding gives $p,q\le U$. Hence all realized prime pairs lie in $P\times P$. The product is finite, and every set of realized prime pairs used below is finite by the subset bound. [given, step 1.1, F3, F4, F5, F6, F7]

2.2 Let $R$ be the subset of $(\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z)\times(\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z)$ consisting of the representations with $p\mid x$. In the finite set from [F2], separate the pairs occurring as first entries of elements of $R$ to form the finite index set $I$. For any set $S$ of indices and predicate $\Phi(s,r)$ on $S\times R$, separate from $S\times\mathcal P(R)$ the pairs $(s,B)$ such that for every $r\in R$, $r\in B$ exactly when $\Phi(s,r)$ holds. For each $s$ the set $B$ exists by separation in $R$, is in $\mathcal P(R)$, and is unique by extensionality. The separated graph is therefore a function with domain $S$ and defines an indexed family of subsets of $R$. [step 1.2, F2, F3, F7, F9]

3.1 Apply step 2.2 with $S=I$ and with the predicate that the first entry of the nested pair is $i$. Write $R_i$ for the resulting fibers. Each $R_i$ is in bijection with the corresponding completion pairs by $(q,y)\mapsto((p,x),(q,y))$ for $i=(p,x)$, with inverse the second entry. Distinct indices give fibers with no common element because the first entry is unique. Each element of $R$ belongs to the fiber of its first entry, and each fiber lies in $R$, so the indexed union is $R$. The sum rule applies to the finite fibers and finite index set, making $R$ finite with $|R|=\sum_{i\in I}|R_i|$ in $\mathbb N$. The bounds are $|R_i|\le C n^{1/16}$ and $|I|\le 6n^{3/4}$ in $\mathbb R$. [step 1.2, step 2.2, F2, F3, F4, F7, F8, F9, F10]

3.2 Prime pairs with $p=2$ are in bijection with a subset of $P$ by keeping the second coordinate, with inverse $q\mapsto(2,q)$. For $q=2$ keep the first coordinate, with inverse $p\mapsto(p,2)$. These inverses recover each ordered pair because both coordinates agree. Thus each of these two sets has at most $U$ elements. [step 1.1, step 2.1, F3, F4, F7]

4.1 Put $d=|I|$ and take an enumeration $e:\{k\in\mathbb N:k<d\}\to I$ by a bijection. Apply the natural-to-real sum identity of [F8] to the enumerated cardinalities, extended by zero beyond the prefix. Real sum monotonicity and the constant-sum formula give $\iota(|R|)=\sum_{k<d}\iota(|R_{e(k)}|)\le \iota(d) C n^{1/16}$. This also holds for $d=0$, when the sums vanish. Since $C n^{1/16}$ is positive, the bound on $\iota(d)$ yields $\iota(|R|)\le 6C n^{13/16}$ by the exponent addition law. Indeed $3/4=12/16$ as rational classes, and adding $1/16$ gives $13/16$. [step 1.1, step 3.1, F3, F4, F6, F8]

5.1 Separate from $P\times P$ the set $J$ of ordered prime pairs that occur in elements of $R$. The set $J$ is finite by step 2.1. Apply step 2.2 with $S=J$ and with the predicate that the two prime coordinates of a representation equal the ordered pair $j$. Write $T_j$ for the resulting fibers. Distinct indices have no common element in their fibers by uniqueness of the two prime coordinates. Each representation belongs to the fiber of its prime pair, and each fiber lies in $R$, so their indexed union is $R$. Each $T_j$ is a finite subset of $R$ and is nonempty by the definition of $J$, so $|T_j|\ge1$. Enumerate $J$ by a bijection from the initial segment of length $|J|$, extending the sequence of part cardinalities by zero beyond that segment. The sum rule, the natural-to-real sum identity and comparison with the constant $1$ on this prefix give $\iota(|J|)\le\iota(|R|)\le6C n^{13/16}$. [step 2.1, step 2.2, step 3.1, step 4.1, F3, F4, F7, F8, F9, F10]

6.1 Interchanging $(p,q,x,y)$ with $(q,p,y,x)$ preserves the equation and takes $p\mid x$ to the condition that the second prime divides the fourth coordinate. Applying the interchange twice is the identity, so the induced map $(p,q)\mapsto(q,p)$ bijects $J$ with the prime pairs having a representation with $q\mid y$. [step 5.1, F3, F5, F7]

7.1 Write $A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4$ for the four finite sets just bounded, in the order of the exclusions in the Statement. Inside each $A_k$, separate the subset $B_k$ of pairs that belong to no $A_\ell$ with $\ell<k$. A pair belongs to the left-associated binary union of the four $A_k$ exactly when it belongs to at least one $A_k$. Its first such index places it in $B_k$, and every member of $B_k$ belongs to $A_k$. Thus the union of the four $B_k$, formed in the same order, equals the union of the four $A_k$. For $k=2,3,4$ the union of the preceding $B_\ell$ is disjoint from $B_k$, since an element of that union lies in some preceding $A_\ell$ and therefore cannot belong to $B_k$. [step 3.2, step 5.1, step 6.1, F7, F9, F10]

8.1 Each $B_k$ is finite and has at most as many elements as $A_k$ by the subset bound. Apply the two-set sum rule at each binary union in step 7.1 and add the four bounds in the real order to obtain at most $12C n^{13/16}+2U$ prime pairs. The rational comparison $1/2<13/16$ follows from $16<26$ by cross multiplication. Since $n\ge1$, monotonicity in the exponent gives $U\le n^{13/16}$ and yields the stated bound. [step 1.1, step 3.2, step 5.1, step 6.1, step 7.1, F3, F4, F6] ∎
