## Statement

Assume the Axiom of Choice. Let $L$ be either the full adelic unit group $B^\times(\mathbb A)$ of the rational Hamilton algebra $B$, or $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb A)$. In the Hamilton case fix a full order $O$ in $B$, a subring containing $1$ with a $\mathbb Z$-basis that is also a $\mathbb Q$-basis of $B$. Give $L=B_{\mathbb R}^\times\times\widehat B^\times$ the product of the real unit topology and the restricted-product topology of the $B_\ell^\times$ relative to $O_\ell^\times$, where $B_\ell=B\otimes_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb Q_\ell$ and $O_\ell=O\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z_\ell$. In the split case use the restricted product of the real and finite general linear groups relative to $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb Z_\ell)$ at the finite places.

In either case let $Z$ consist of the scalar multiplications by ideles in $\mathbb A^\times=\mathbb R^\times\times\mathbb A_f^\times$. Give the group $P=L/Z$ the quotient topology and write $q:L\to P$ for its projection. Let $\widetilde\Gamma$ be the diagonal image of $B^\times$ or $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb Q)$, respectively, and put $\Gamma=q(\widetilde\Gamma)$. Give the two sets of cosets $X_L=\widetilde\Gamma Z\backslash L$ and $X_P=\Gamma\backslash P$ their quotient topologies. Then
$$\Phi:X_L\longrightarrow X_P,\qquad \widetilde\Gamma Zg\longmapsto\Gamma q(g)$$
is a homeomorphism of locally compact Hausdorff spaces and $\Phi(xg)=\Phi(x)q(g)$.

Equip the two spaces with their Borel sigma-algebras. For a Borel measure, right invariance means that translating any Borel set on the right by any acting group element leaves its measure unchanged. Given a right-invariant Borel measure $\nu$ on $X_P$, put $\mu(E)=\nu(\Phi(E))$ for every Borel $E\subseteq X_L$. Then $\mu$ is right invariant and its pushforward is $\nu$, meaning $\mu(\Phi^{-1}(A))=\nu(A)$ for every Borel $A\subseteq X_P$. Transport by $\Phi^{-1}$ gives the inverse correspondence.

Let $R_L(g)u(x)=u(xg)$ and $R_P(p)f(y)=f(yp)$ act on the respective complex $L^2$ spaces of square-integrable functions modulo equality almost everywhere. These actions are by bijective linear isometries. Pullback $Tf=f\circ\Phi$ is a bijective complex-linear isometry satisfying $R_L(g)T=TR_P(q(g))$. For corresponding Radon measures, both actions are strongly continuous unitary representations. For any corresponding right-invariant Borel measures, a closed invariant subspace on either side corresponds to a closed invariant subspace on the other, and the two restricted actions are strongly continuous together. A unitary constituent is the restricted unitary representation on such a nonzero subspace. The correspondence preserves irreducibility, complex linear dimension and the fixed vectors for every subgroup of $L$ and its image in $P$. Complex linear dimension is the cardinality of a complex linear basis, with the same convention in infinite dimension. On the $L$ side every scalar acts trivially.

## Proof

**Given:** The two adelic group constructions, the Axiom of Choice and the right-invariant Borel measure on either quotient.

**Goal:** Identify the quotient spaces, the right actions on square-integrable functions and their unitary constituents.

1.1 In the Hamilton case the product of the two scalar quotient maps from [F1] is continuous, open by the rectangle basis, and surjective. Its kernel is $\mathbb R^\times\times\mathbb A_f^\times=Z$, viewed as central scalar multiplications. By the first isomorphism theorem the product map induces a group isomorphism from $P$ onto the full projective Hamilton group. It is continuous by the quotient topology on $P$ and open because the image of an open subset of $P$ is the image of its open inverse image in $L$ under the open product map. The rational image is the diagonal projective rational subgroup. In the split case the corresponding group projection and its kernel are supplied by [F2]. Hence in both cases $P$ is a topological group and $q$ is an open continuous quotient homomorphism. [F1, F2, F3]

1.2 To verify the dimension convention, take any two complex linear bases $U,V$ of a complex vector space. If one basis is finite, their sizes agree by [F9]. Otherwise both are infinite. Each vector of $V$ has a finite expansion in $U$ by spanning, and the expansion is unique by linear independence. These finite supports cover $U$. If a vector of $U$ were absent from all these supports, the span of $V$ would lie in the span of the remaining vectors of $U$, contrary to independence of $U$. Well order both bases, and for each vector of $U$ take the least vector of $V$ whose support contains it. The chosen vector of $V$ together with the position in its ordered finite support gives an injection $U\to V\times\mathbb N$. The latter injects into $V\times V$ because for each natural number $n$ we can take the element at position $n$ in the well-order of $V$, starting at position zero.
